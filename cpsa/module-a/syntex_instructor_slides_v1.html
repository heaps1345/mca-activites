Penetration Testing Lifecycle

CPSA Objective A1 • Pre-Engagement Essentials

A1 • Content

The Six Phases of a Penetration Test

Phase 01
Pre-Engagement
Scope, RoE, legal authority, emergency contacts
Phase 02
Reconnaissance
OSINT, passive intel, attack surface mapping
Phase 03
Scanning & Enum
Port scans, service versions, vulnerability discovery
Phase 04
Exploitation
Controlled exploitation within agreed scope only
Phase 05
Post-Exploitation
Lateral movement, privilege escalation, persistence check
Phase 06
Reporting
Executive summary, technical findings, remediation roadmap
Pre-Engagement is never skippable.
Without a signed RoE and explicit written permission, any test activity is potentially a criminal offence under the Computer Misuse Act 1990.
  • Rules of Engagement (RoE) — the legally binding document covering scope, timing, methods, and emergency contacts
  • Scope Definition — explicit IP ranges, hostnames, and URLs. If it is not in scope, do not touch it
  • Note-taking — timestamped, contemporaneous logs; essential for the report and your legal defence
A1 • Real-World Analogy

The Building Surveyor

"A building surveyor doesn't just turn up and start poking at walls. They arrive with a signed letter of authority from the building owner, a defined scope of work, and a method statement. The first thing they do is introduce themselves to site management and log their arrival."

Pre-Engagement Parallel

How it maps to your test

Signed letter of authority = Permission to Test letter in the engagement pack. Without it, you are trespassing.

Defined scope of work = The RoE. Walls in the building = IP ranges and hosts. The surveyor does not test the neighbouring building.

Log arrival with site management = Notify the emergency contact at test start. If alarms go off, they know who it was.

Method statement = The testing methodology and agreed tools list. No surprises for the client.

Topic A2

UK Legal Framework

CPSA Objective A2 • Computer Misuse Act 1990

A2 • Content

Computer Misuse Act 1990 — The Three Offences

  • Section 1 — Unauthorised Access
    Accessing any computer without permission. Max: 2 years / unlimited fine. This is why written scope matters. Testing an out-of-scope host = S1 offence, full stop.
  • Section 2 — Access with Intent to Commit Further Offences
    Accessing a system with intent to commit another crime (e.g. fraud, theft). Max: 5 years / unlimited fine. Intent is the aggravating factor.
  • Section 3 — Unauthorised Modification
    Modifying data without permission. Includes uploading malware, deleting files, changing passwords. Max: 10 years / unlimited fine. Even a test payload left behind can be S3.
The Consultant's Defence
A valid written authorisation (RoE + Permission to Test letter) is your legal defence. It does not make the act legal by itself; it demonstrates the access was authorised and therefore not an offence.

Police and Justice Act 2006 — also covers creation/supply of articles likely to be used for CMA offences. Be careful what tools you distribute.
Key exam point:
The CMA applies to any computer system in the UK, regardless of where the attacker is located. Jurisdiction follows the victim's location.
A2 • Real-World Analogy

The Locksmith

"A locksmith can pick any lock. The skill is identical whether they are helping a locked-out homeowner or breaking into someone's house. The difference is entirely the authorisation. The police do not arrest the locksmith for knowing how — they arrest them for doing it without permission."

Computer Misuse Act Parallel

The three CMA sections as locksmithing

Section 1 = picking the lock to walk into someone's house without being asked. The act itself is the offence.

Section 2 = picking the lock because you intend to steal the TV. The intent makes it worse.

Section 3 = picking the lock and then changing it so the owner cannot get back in. Modification is the aggravating act.

The signed RoE = the homeowner standing next to you with a written note saying "I asked this person to pick my lock."

Topic A3

Scoping & Engagement Types

CPSA Objective A3 • Black / Grey / White Box • Cloud Rules

A3 • Content

Black, Grey, and White Box Testing

  • Black Box — Zero prior knowledge. Simulates an external attacker. Most realistic threat model, but slowest and most expensive. OSINT phase is critical.
  • Grey Box — Partial knowledge. Typical for most commercial engagements. May include network diagrams, credentials, or architecture docs. Better ROI for client.
  • White Box — Full knowledge. Source code, network diagrams, admin credentials. Maximum coverage; identifies issues a black-box test would miss entirely.
Cloud Permission Rule (critical exam topic):
To test cloud-hosted infrastructure you need written permission from both the client AND the cloud provider. AWS, Azure, and GCP all have penetration testing policies; breaching them can result in account suspension and legal action against you personally. Always check the provider's policy first.
Scope Creep Warning
Clients often add items mid-engagement: "While you're at it, can you look at our AWS portal?" This is scope creep. Any addition requires a written change request and updated RoE. Never extend scope verbally.
  • Social engineering and phishing must be explicitly included in scope — they cannot be assumed
  • Physical access testing requires its own separate authorisation document
  • Denial of service testing is almost always excluded — confirm in writing
A3 • Real-World Analogy

The Security Audit at a Rented Office

"A company rents a floor in a serviced office building. You're asked to test their security. The tenant can authorise you to test their floor — but they cannot authorise you to test the building's shared network infrastructure, the lifts, or another tenant's server rack. That permission belongs to the building owner."

Cloud Shared Responsibility Parallel

Why this maps to cloud exactly

The tenant = your client. They authorise testing of their resources: their VMs, their application, their S3 buckets.

The building owner = AWS / Azure / GCP. They own the hypervisor, the network fabric, the physical hardware. Only they can authorise testing of the underlying infrastructure.

The other tenants = other cloud customers sharing the same physical host. A misconfigured test can affect them. This is why providers have strict pentest policies.

Without the landlord's permission = potential violation of the provider's acceptable use policy, account suspension, and personal legal liability.

Topic A4

Risk Management & CVSS

CPSA Objective A4 • CVSS v3.1 Scoring • Severity Bands

A4 • Content

CVSS v3.1 — Severity Bands and Score Groups

Severity Score Range Typical Impact Report Priority
Critical9.0 – 10.0Full system compromise, no auth requiredImmediate escalation to client
High7.0 – 8.9Significant impact, may require auth or user interactionFix within 30 days
Medium4.0 – 6.9Limited impact, requires complex conditionsFix within 90 days
Low0.1 – 3.9Minimal impact, informational concernBest effort
Info0.0No direct impact, observation onlyAwareness only
Three Score Groups: Base (the vulnerability in isolation, does not change) / Temporal (exploit availability, patch status — changes over time) / Environmental (organisation-specific context — same CVE can score differently at different clients).
Key exam distinction:
The same vulnerability can have a different CVSS score at different organisations. An unauthenticated RCE on a dev server with no external access scores lower than the same vuln on a public-facing payment processor. The Environmental score adjusts for this.
  • Attack Vector (AV) — most commonly mis-scored; Network vs. Adjacent vs. Local vs. Physical
  • Scope (S) — Changed vs. Unchanged; does exploitation affect components beyond the vulnerable one?
  • Attack Complexity (AC) — Low vs. High; does the attacker need specific race conditions or special config?
A4 • Real-World Analogy

The Broken Window in Two Buildings

"A broken ground-floor window is a Critical finding in a jewellery shop and a Low finding in a storage unit in a remote industrial estate. The window is the same window. The score changes because the context changes."

Environmental Score Parallel

Base vs. Environmental scores

Base score = the window is broken. That fact never changes. It is a physical access risk regardless of location.

Environmental score at the jeweller = high-value assets, public-facing, frequent footfall, manned by staff. An attacker reaching this window has enormous impact. Score goes up.

Environmental score at the storage unit = low-value assets, remote location, CCTV, steel containers inside. An attacker reaching this window still has limited impact. Score stays low.

Lesson: Never tell a client their CVSS score without the environmental context. A blanket "9.8 Critical" on a dev box no one can reach from the internet is misleading and erodes trust.

Topic A5

Professional Reporting

CPSA Objective A5 • Report Structure • Secure Delivery • Data Retention

A5 • Content

Report Structure, Secure Delivery, and Data Retention

  • Executive Summary — written for the board and senior management. No technical jargon. Business impact, risk posture, overall risk rating, top 3 recommendations. One page.
  • Technical Findings — written for the IT team. Each finding includes: title, CVSS score, description, evidence (screenshots/output), affected systems, remediation steps, compliance mapping.
  • Remediation Roadmap — prioritised fix list, categorised by severity and effort. Gives the client a clear action plan rather than just a list of problems.
  • Secure Delivery — encrypted email (password via separate channel) or a secure client portal only. Never send a pentest report as a plain-text email attachment.
  • Data Retention — industry standard is 6–12 months. The RoE should specify the agreed retention period and destruction method. Client data is your legal liability while you hold it.
Audience separation is everything.
A board member who receives a 60-page technical document and cannot find the business risk summary will lose confidence in you. A sysadmin who receives only a one-line risk statement cannot action anything.

Write two audiences, two sections, one report.
Evidence tools:
Screenshots with timestamps, tool output captured to file, network capture (pcap) where relevant. Everything timestamped, everything contemporaneous. Reconstructed evidence has no legal standing.
A5 • Real-World Analogy

The Doctor and the Patient

"When a consultant tells a patient 'You have a moderate blockage in the left anterior descending artery affecting myocardial perfusion with a fractional flow reserve of 0.74,' the patient nods and understands nothing. When they say 'Your heart isn't getting enough blood to one section. We need to fix this in the next few weeks or you risk a heart attack,' the patient can make a decision."

Executive Summary vs. Technical Report Parallel

The same finding, two audiences

Technical finding: "CVE-2021-44228, Log4Shell RCE, CVSS 10.0, affecting Apache 2.14.1 on 10.10.0.50:8080. JNDI lookup via User-Agent header confirmed via out-of-band DNS callback."

Executive summary: "A critical vulnerability was found that allows any attacker on the internet to take complete control of your public web server. This is a known, actively exploited flaw. It must be patched this week."

The rule: If your executive summary requires technical knowledge to understand, rewrite it. If your technical finding lacks evidence and reproduction steps, rewrite that too.
