Email Header Analysis

Email headers record the full journey of a message through the internet. Analysing them reveals originating IP addresses, mail server infrastructure, and whether anti-spoofing controls like SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are correctly configured.

Part 1 - Anatomy of Email Headers

Email headers are read bottom to top - the first hop is at the bottom, the final delivery is at the top. Each mail server in the chain adds a Received: header as the email passes through.

HeaderPurposeRecon Value
Received:Added by each mail server in the chain - shows IP and timestamp at each hopReveals true originating IP (bottom Received header)
From:Display name and address shown to recipientCan be spoofed - compare with Return-Path
Return-Path:Where bounce messages are sent - set by sending serverHarder to spoof than From: - mismatch = suspicious
X-Originating-IP:Original client IP (added by some mail services)Reveals sender's real IP before any relay
Message-ID:Unique ID assigned by originating mail serverDomain after @ reveals sending infrastructure
DKIM-Signature:Cryptographic signature from sending domainValid DKIM = domain verified; missing = potential spoof
Authentication-Results:Receiving server's SPF/DKIM/DMARC check resultsSummarises whether anti-spoofing passed or failed
Part 2 - Analysing a Sample Header
1
Read and trace the Received chain

Analyse the sample email header below. Read it from the bottom upward to trace the email's journey.

-- SAMPLE EMAIL HEADER (read bottom to top) --

Authentication-Results: mx.syntexsolutions.net;
       spf=fail (domain of ceo@syntexsolutions.net does not designate
       192.168.77.5 as permitted sender)
       dkim=none;
       dmarc=fail

Received: from mail.syntexsolutions.net (20.24.202.6)
    by mx.syntexsolutions.net; Tue, 10 Oct 2025 09:21:33 +0000

Received: from smtp-relay.phishingdomain.com (192.168.77.5)
    by mail.syntexsolutions.net; Tue, 10 Oct 2025 09:21:31 +0000

X-Originating-IP: 203.0.113.42

Message-ID: <abc123@smtp-relay.phishingdomain.com>

DKIM-Signature: (none)

Return-Path: <bounce@phishingdomain.com>

From: CEO Sarah Chen <ceo@syntexsolutions.net>

To: finance@syntexsolutions.net

Subject: URGENT: Wire transfer required today
⚠
This header shows a classic Business Email Compromise (BEC) spoof. The From: shows the CEO's real address, but SPF fails, DKIM is absent, DMARC fails, Return-Path points to a different domain, and Message-ID was generated by a third-party relay. All four signals indicate this is a spoofed email.

2
Check SPF, DKIM and DMARC records via DNS

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are published as DNS TXT records. During recon you can check whether a target domain has these controls in place - absence of any of them means the domain can be spoofed.

-- SPF record: published on the sending domain --
dig TXT syntex-solutions.com | grep "v=spf"

-- DMARC record: published at _dmarc subdomain --
dig TXT _dmarc.syntex-solutions.com

-- DKIM: published at selector._domainkey subdomain --
-- Selector name is in the DKIM-Signature header (s= tag) --
dig TXT selector1._domainkey.syntex-solutions.com
RecordWhat it doesDangerous if missing
SPFLists which mail servers are authorised to send for the domainDomain can be spoofed from any server
DKIMCryptographically signs each email so the recipient can verify itEmails cannot be verified as genuine
DMARCPolicy for what to do when SPF/DKIM fail (none/quarantine/reject)No enforcement even if SPF and DKIM are present
🎯
CREST Exam Focus: Know the purpose of SPF, DKIM, and DMARC and their DNS record locations. A domain with no DMARC record set to p=reject is spoofable even if SPF and DKIM exist, because DMARC is the enforcement mechanism.

Q2

What does it mean when the Return-Path header domain differs from the From: header domain?

A mismatch between Return-Path and From: indicates the email was likely sent from a different mail server than the domain it claims to be from - a strong indicator of email spoofing. The Return-Path is set by the sending mail server and is harder to forge. When it points to a different domain than the From: address, the email is almost certainly not genuinely from the stated sender. This pattern is common in Business Email Compromise and phishing attacks.
Q3

What DNS record type and subdomain format is used to publish a DMARC policy for domain.com?

Q4

A target domain has SPF and DKIM correctly configured, but no DMARC record. Can the domain still be spoofed? Explain your reasoning.

Yes, the domain can still be effectively spoofed. SPF and DKIM are authentication mechanisms - they tell the receiving mail server whether to trust the email. But without DMARC, there is no enforcement policy instructing the receiving server what to DO when SPF or DKIM fails. Without DMARC, most mail servers will still deliver the email to the inbox even if SPF fails, because they have no policy to follow. DMARC provides the enforcement: p=quarantine sends suspicious mail to spam, p=reject drops it entirely. SPF and DKIM without DMARC is like having a lock without a door frame.
Q5

What authentication mechanism uses a cryptographic signature added to email headers to verify the sending domain?

Lab Complete - Key Takeaways: Read email headers bottom to top to trace message flow. The bottom Received header and X-Originating-IP reveal the true sender IP. Return-Path mismatch with From: is a spoofing indicator. SPF restricts permitted sending servers, DKIM provides cryptographic verification, and DMARC enforces what happens when they fail. A domain missing DMARC p=reject is spoofable regardless of SPF/DKIM. MXToolbox provides quick online analysis of all three records.
